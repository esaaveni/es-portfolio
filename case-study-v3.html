Context

At LATAM, a fundamental strategic tension exists between revenue management and operational efficiency.

While non-refundable fares are essential for driving airline revenue, they become a massive source of call center friction when passengers fail to recognize the limitations of their ticket.

Why this matters ✕

Non-refundable fares are the mechanism behind cheap tickets: they give airlines revenue certainty at the moment of booking, which is what makes discounted prices possible. That certainty has a price: US airlines collected $2.8 billion in cancellation fees in 2019, and still above $1 billion in 2023, even after most major carriers had eliminated change fees.

Bureau of Transportation Statistics · Cancellation Fees by Airline

Or we, as designers, fail to make those limitations recognizable.

Making these conditions clear isn’t just a UX goal; it dictates our call center costs. And the confusion always starts at the exact same place: the eligibility screen.

About the airline

LATAM is the largest airline in Latin America and the 10th largest globally by seat capacity, transporting a record 87.4 million passengers in 2025, averaging nearly 250,000 travelers daily.

Problem

Our customer satisfaction surveys showed a sustained decline, heavily concentrated in a single category: refund policies.

Our data scientist and I went full Batman and Robin on the call logs and unburied a single behavioral pattern: 67% of passengers with non-reimbursable fares were calling the contact center specifically to understand why they weren't receiving a full refund.

Light fare tier

Light fare holders alone accounted for 37% of all inbound calls — the highest volume of any fare class.

This friction triggered the moment they hit the eligibility screen, which functioned as a compliance shield and backend loading gate rather than an informational tool.

Why the screen worked this way ✕

Because our post-purchase architecture was completely disconnected from the booking flow, Engineering used this screen as a forced delay to fetch fare rules, while Legal used it to clear liability. Neither system was optimized for clarity. I call this “business-centric design.”

Some passengers were so paralyzed by the vague, heavy warnings that they abandoned the digital flow entirely, terrified that clicking “Next” would instantly forfeit their money.

Solution

hypothesis

We believe that displaying personalized refund amounts directly on the eligibility page

will result in

higher-quality traffic in the refund flow, providing passengers with clear fare conditions and minimizing redundant support calls.

fig.02 · refund flow diagram - after · change of the system queries to the Global Distribution System (GDS)

By shifting the “moment of truth” to the start of the flow, we aligned expectations before the user invested further effort. The design answered three questions immediately upon entering: how much, what, and why.

The design didn’t soften the bad news — it delivered it with a number attached, on the first screen.

How much? The exact, personalized monetary value appeared at the top of the screen — not buried after multiple taps.

What is it? The UI clarified that the amount was a return of boarding taxes, not a partial ticket refund.

Why? Dynamic cards connected the limited refund amount to the user’s past purchase decision, reinforcing the specific fare conditions of their ticket.

Cognitive recall: The brand color of the user’s fare tier was integrated into the UI component — matching visual cues used across all LATAM services, giving users an intuitive way to recognize the ticket class they purchased.

Test scope

The A/B test was deployed globally using a Group Sequential approach — a statistical method that pre-defines specific checkpoints for reviewing results, so you can stop early if the evidence is strong enough without inflating the chance of a false positive.

This prevented the error of “peeking”: reviewing results prematurely and making rushed decisions based on incomplete data.

fig.04 · A/B testing · control vs treatment · ABSmartly

Metric framework

Operating under the principle of “if you can’t trust the data, use proxies, not assumptions,” we established a three-tier metric hierarchy:

Primary · proxy for clarity

RequestToCompleteRefundConversion (baseline: 61.6%) — of all passengers who reached the refund details screen, how many actually completed the request? An increase here would prove that users at this stage were better informed and no longer abandoning in frustration.

Secondary · behavioral context

Traffic from the eligibility screen to the calculation screen, and redirections toward the voluntary exchange flow. FullStory observations revealed passengers exploring exchanges — a behavior worth monitoring.

Guardrail · break glass

FullRefundCompleteConversion: if this moved in the wrong direction, stop immediately. It ensured the new filtering logic did not accidentally block legitimate, fully refundable transactions.

Challenge

LATAM operates across dozens of markets, each with different tax structures, fare rules, and legal requirements. Every combination needed to return the right number in real time.

To present an accurate “total to refund” on the eligibility screen, the engine had to instantaneously aggregate the ticket price, fare conditions, and embarkation fees — which are often the only refundable component of a “Zero Refund” ticket.

Logic complexity

The automated pre-check was not a simple binary filter. It required a robust logic layer to exclude cases that did not fit the standard non-refundable profile:

Flex fares and contingency cases — where specific override rules apply.

Legal mandates — such as the “Ley de retracto” (right of withdrawal) in specific jurisdictions.

Commercial exceptions and award bookings — tickets purchased via mileage or marketing exceptions.

The implementation revealed significant functional misunderstandings about the exact audience parameters. This led to a rollback, a more exhaustive analysis, and a re-launch. The lesson: technical bottlenecks in high-logic flows can only be mitigated by a shared understanding of the intended use case across Design, Development, and Product.

Takeaways

This experiment represents a shift toward rigorous experimental validation — away from assumptions and toward behavioral proxies, experiment integrity, and collaborative OKRs.

The proxy mandate

When primary data is noisy or lacks traceability, use behavioral proxies. “Refund completion rate” stood in for “passenger understanding” — because understanding is invisible, but behavior isn’t.

Experiment integrity

Statistical drift happens when you leave an experiment running too long after the result is clear — new users dilute the signal, and the result can shift in ways that no longer reflect the real effect.

Collaborative OKRs

There is a clear need to propose more sensitive ways of measuring user experience beyond traditional, low-response metrics like CSAT or claims volume.

Conclusion

Positive friction at the start of the journey reduces operational friction at the end of it. The “Zero Refund” experiment demonstrates that immediate, personalized transparency is a high-leverage strategy for both passenger satisfaction and institutional efficiency.
