Context

LATAM Airlines operates across 15 markets in Latin America. The Customer Service squad is responsible for the digital self-service experience — the flows passengers use to change, cancel, or get a refund on their tickets, without calling support.

The voluntary exchange flow was one of those self-service options. Passengers who found it could change their ticket end-to-end inside the app. The product worked. The problem was that on mobile, almost no one was reaching it.

This was the squad’s first ever production A/B experiment. There was no internal playbook, no precedent for how to design the measurement framework. The constraint going in: mobile only, one change, and whatever we built had to be verifiable by the business.

Problem

The Medallia detractor data was specific. Passengers weren’t writing vague complaints — they were writing the same sentence, week after week: “It was hard to find where to change the ticket.” Ten percent of mobile users, in their own words, unprompted.

The BigQuery analysis made it measurable. In a sample week, 268,210 mobile sessions reached My Trips Level 2 — the panel where exchange should have been reachable. Only 2.45% got to the Request Change page. On desktop that same week, 12.33% made it.

fig.01 · access rate to exchange flow by device · My Trips Level 2 → Request Change

The gap wasn’t behavioral. It was architectural.

FullStory sessions confirmed it. Passengers were scanning the My Trips panel, tapping adjacent buttons, backing out — not because the feature was broken, but because there was no direct entry point. Users who needed exchange were routing through the baggage store as a discovery workaround.

The flow worked fine once you reached it. The fix wasn’t a redesign. It was an entry.

Solution

hypothesis

Adding a direct “Exchange & Refunds” entry in the My Trips button panel on mobile

will result in

a measurable increase in organic traffic to the voluntary exchange flow, without cannibalizing the baggage flow.

Version A was the existing My Trips layout. Version B added one new button: a direct path to “Exchange & Refunds,” mobile only.

I made a deliberate decision not to touch anything else — not the button styling, not the surrounding hierarchy, not the desktop experience. The discipline matters because it makes the result attributable. If you redesign the panel and traffic goes up, you don’t know why. If you add one entry and traffic goes up, you know exactly why.

This was non-negotiable when I framed the experiment brief.

Test scope

The A/B test was deployed on mobile only, across all 15 LATAM markets, using ABSmartly for experiment management. Traffic was split equally between Version A (control) and Version B (treatment).

The experiment used predefined checkpoints — a statistical method that lets a team stop an experiment early without inflating the risk of a false positive, as long as the stopping rules are declared before the experiment starts. The read date and 95% confidence threshold were locked in the experiment brief before any code was written.

This prevented the error of “peeking”: reviewing results prematurely and making decisions based on incomplete data. Defining the stopping criteria upfront is what made the early close legitimate.

Metric framework

Before we wrote a line of code, I defined three tiers. The discipline of defining metrics before results exist is what removes the debate when results come in.

Primary · the behavior we wanted to move

Unique pageviews to the voluntary-change landing page from mobile. If the new entry worked, this number would go up. Everything else followed from this signal.

Secondary · downstream evidence

Unique pageviews to exchange confirmation, and the CSAT shift on the “hard to find” detractor segment — evidence that access improvement translated into completed exchanges and reduced frustration.

Guardrail · what “harm” looked like

Mobile baggage-store access. The hypothesis was that a direct exchange path would not cannibalize the bags flow. Declaring it as a guardrail before the test meant there was no debate about the 7.98% drop when results came in. We’d already agreed on what harm looked like — and this wasn’t it.

Results

Statistical confidence reached 95% four days ahead of the planned read date. The experiment closed early — the predefined checkpoint logic made that call legitimate.

The −7.98% baggage-store drop is counter-intuitive. Users who previously routed through the bags page to find exchange no longer needed to. They weren’t buying fewer bags — they were no longer using the bags page as a map. Conversion on baggage purchases was unaffected.

CRO Brasil published the experiment results. The case was named finalist in the CRO Awards Brasil 2025, “Experiments that move the needle” category.

Challenge

This was the squad’s first production experiment. There was no internal playbook. No one had run an A/B test in this product surface before.

I built the measurement framework from scratch — BigQuery queries, ABSmartly configuration, predefined checkpoint logic. The upfront investment was significant. But the cost showed up in the readout: when the results came in clean, no one questioned them. The rigor removed the debate.

The baggage-store drop created a moment of friction during the readout. A 7.98% decrease in any metric draws attention. Because we had declared it as a guardrail before the experiment started — and defined what “harm” meant in advance — there was no debate. We had already agreed on the answer.

Takeaways

This experiment shipped as a one-button change. What it actually shipped was a methodology. The three PM squads that ran experiments after this one used the same framework: primary, secondary, guardrail — defined before code, not after results.

Evidence is cheaper than opinion

One A/B test changes how a team argues about everything else. Before the experiment, “we should make this more findable” was a design opinion. After it, there was a number. The opinion didn’t change. The cost of dismissing it did.

The discovery layer is the design

When users can’t find the feature, no improvement to the feature itself will help. Findability isn’t a navigation problem — it’s a design surface. Treating it that way meant we measured it the way we’d measure any other design decision: with a hypothesis, a metric, and a test.

Cross-functional permission is granted by results

Design earned a seat in OKR co-authoring after this experiment, not before. The invitation came because the squad produced evidence the business could read and trust. Influence followed accountability — not the other way around.

Conclusion

After the results published internally, design was invited into OKR co-authoring for the first time. Not because someone decided designers deserved a seat. Because the experiment produced evidence the business could verify.

The methodology became the template for every experiment the squad ran afterward. Three PM squads adopted the primary / secondary / guardrail framework directly. This case is the one peers cited when arguing for an experimentation practice — not because of the result, but because of how the result was produced.
