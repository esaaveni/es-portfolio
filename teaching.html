Semana de Inserción Laboral · PUCV 2026

In 2026 the e[ad] School of Architecture and Design at Pontificia Universidad Católica de Valparaíso invited me to speak at their Labor Insertion Week, a program that prepares students and recent graduates for the job market. Around thirty designers and architects in the room, this time in Spanish.

My talk: Cómo cobrar por mi trabajo de diseño. The least-taught skill in design school. We covered real salary ranges by discipline, what a gross salary actually leaves in your pocket, the tax mechanics of freelancing in Chile, and how to read the employment ecosystem: corporations, startups, agencies, and freelance, each with different rules.

Lectures

While leading UX design at Lead Innovation in Bangkok, I taught as a guest lecturer at Bangkok University’s School of Entrepreneurship and Management (BUSEM): sessions of around thirty students, taught in English to Thai and international classes.

The lectures covered the ground between design and business: customer experience design and stakeholder mapping. Teaching entrepreneurship students rather than design students shaped the material. They didn’t need tool tutorials; they needed to understand why experience decisions are business decisions.

Focused workshops

Alongside the lectures, I ran hands-on workshops organized by the BUSEM Center for Entrepreneurship (BCE). The format was deliberately small, thirty seats booked in advance, so every participant left with work reviewed, not just notes taken.

The logo design workshop (September 2019) took students from brief to presented concept in a single session. It opened with what a visual identity actually is, argued through the Beats acquisition, then moved to positioning, sketches, and critique throughout. The constraint of one afternoon forced the same discipline I use in product work: decide what problem the design solves before touching form.

Meet the Experts

BUSEM’s “Meet the Experts” program paired student founders with practitioners for one-on-one consulting sessions. I held the User Experience / Digital Product Design seat. Students brought their ventures, and we worked through their actual product problems: onboarding flows, service design, how to test an idea before building it.

This was mentoring at its most honest: no curriculum, just a founder, their product, and an hour to find what was actually broken underneath.

Why it matters

Teaching is the fastest test of whether you actually understand your own practice. Explaining stakeholder mapping to a room of twenty-year-old founders, in their second language and mine, strips away every piece of jargon a design process hides behind. What survives that translation is the part that was true.

The mentoring sessions ran in the same direction as my product work: diagnose before prescribing. Most founders arrived asking for a better interface. Most left with a clearer problem.
