Context

Krungthai Bank (KTB) is one of Thailand’s largest banks — state-owned, serving tens of millions of customers. In 2017 the bank was executing a digital transformation: not updating an existing app, but rebuilding its entire NetBank experience from the ground up, across mobile and web simultaneously.

Strategy agency DINN had delivered the upstream work: customer research, segmentation, a digital banking strategy, and a brand foundation. Lead Innovation was brought in to translate that upstream work into product. Research reports don’t move money. Someone has to turn strategic findings into an information architecture, screen flows, interaction rules, and a system developers can actually build — and then prove with real users that the translation worked. That was our job.

The scope

The project covered the entire KTB NetBank product. Nine account types. Funds transfer to own accounts, third-party accounts, other banks, and internationally — via account list, manual input, QR code, and PromptPay. Bill payment with full vendor management. Investment products: fund subscription, redemption, switching, NAV, and portfolio summary. Loans, cheques, and services including Western Union, ATM and branch management, and a queue-at-branch feature.

Mobile and web were scoped simultaneously, with explicit parity decisions on every function. Some features ran on both. Some were web-only. Some mobile-only. These decisions were made at the architecture stage — not discovered in QA.

The Terms of Reference formalised the brief: defined deliverables, phased milestones, and a December 2016 customer profile dataset as a structured input alongside DINN’s strategy work. This was a full product architecture assignment, not a redesign of one flow.

Architecture

Two candidate information architectures were developed against DINN’s research — Map A and Map B. Both were built from the same core insight: Thai banking users’ mental model does not map neatly onto bank product categories. People think in tasks, not product lines.

The new architecture prioritised actions over products. The home screen organised around what users need to do: check balance, move money, pay bills. Products — loans, investment funds, fixed accounts — lived in a separate navigation layer accessed when needed, not interleaved with daily tasks. The IA was sketched by hand on paper against the research first. Making the structural argument early, when changing it was free, is the only way to avoid rebuilding it in code.

We treated DINN’s findings as constraints, not suggestions. Every IA decision had to trace back to a research finding. Where the research was silent, we marked it as an assumption to be tested rather than guessing silently. The usability protocol existed precisely to catch those marked gaps.

Transaction flows

Four major transaction flows were designed end-to-end, documented as annotated diagrams that served as the contract between design and development. Each annotation answered a question a developer would otherwise have to guess.

Send Money was the highest-stakes flow. The one customers use most and trust least. The flow covered sending to KTB accounts, third-party accounts, other banks, PromptPay, and wallet — each with its own account selection path, recipient verification, and confirmation state. Every screen state and every decision branch was documented in a single diagram.

Pay Bills covered the full vendor lookup, payee management, and payment flow — adding new payees, paying from a saved favourites list, and the confirmation and success states for each path.

Pre-login — registration, login, and forgot password — was delivered as Sprint 1. The first experience a new user has with the bank sets the trust tone for everything that follows. It received the same flow rigour as the transaction screens.

Product navigation mapped how users move between the banking home, investment products, and loan services — including the transitions that had no equivalent in the previous on-premise experience and had to be designed from first principles.

Habit-forming features

In 2017, Thai banking customers didn’t yet trust digital payments for daily use. Branch and ATM were the default. KTB’s strategy included features designed specifically to change that behaviour — to give users a reason to open the app beyond moving money, and to build the kind of repeated use that eventually becomes trust.

Dreams

Savings goals where the bank surfaces a suggested amount drawn from the user’s own cash flow. Users set a goal, name it, track progress, and receive contextual suggestions based on actual spending patterns — not generic savings calculators.

Lifestyle

Curated offers and recommendations connected to account activity. The idea was to bridge banking and daily life: the app knows what you spend on, so it can surface things that are genuinely relevant rather than generic promotions.

Rewards

A recognition system rewarding active use of digital banking features, including a leaderboard. Designed to surface the value of going digital to users who still defaulted to branch or ATM.

Butler

A personalised assistant layer surfacing contextual actions based on account status, upcoming obligations, and usage patterns. Early version of what is now called proactive banking.

These features were speculative at the time. In 2024, savings goals with contextual suggestions, lifestyle offers, and proactive assistants are standard in the neobank playbook. KTB designed them in 2017.

Validation

Two validation methods ran in sequence: a heuristic evaluation against the first design pass, then two-round moderated usability testing against the working design.

The heuristic evaluation was run before involving real users — using established usability principles to identify structural problems while they were still cheap to fix. Issues caught here were resolved before the usability protocol began.

The usability protocol covered two days. Day one: icon findability, registration, login, look and feel, sending money to others, and payments. Day two: transfers to own accounts (including adding an account and scheduling a transaction), top-up, and paying from favourites.

Findings were graded on a declared severity scale set before testing began:

Critical

The app doesn’t work if this is ignored. Must be solved.

Important

It works, but it’s genuinely difficult to use. Solve to improve performance and satisfaction.

Basic

Doesn’t affect functionality, but annoys users and erodes satisfaction.

Measured outcomes per task: completion rate, average time, and a satisfaction questionnaire covering label comprehension, language, navigation flow, interactive elements, and system feedback. A second round focused specifically on the critical issues from round one and verified the design enhancements resolved them.

System

Two guideline sets made the work survive its handoff. An icon system built on the Material Design grid — product icons and system icons, delivered in light and dark background variants, documented to the component level with usage rules. And a system feedback specification: what the app says, and how, at every error, pending, and success state.

In banking, the success path is table stakes. Trust is decided in the pending screens, failed transfers, and recovery paths. Specifying system feedback as a first-class deliverable — not a development afterthought — is the difference between an app that works and one people trust with their salary.

Takeaways

Research is a constraint system

Two IA maps were developed and the structural argument was made early, against actual research findings. Every decision traceable to a finding; every gap marked as a testable assumption. The alternative is decorating your own opinions with someone else’s research.

Parity decisions are design decisions

Which features run on mobile, web, or both is not a technical call. Every parity gap is a promise — or a broken promise — about the consistency of the digital experience. Making those decisions explicit at the IA stage, rather than discovering them in QA, is what keeps the product coherent.

Declare severity before you test

Agreeing on what “critical” means before seeing any findings removes the negotiation afterward. The same discipline — define the evaluation before the evidence exists — became the backbone of my experimentation practice years later.

Error states are the product

The success path is table stakes. Trust is decided in the pending screens, failed transfers, and recovery paths. This is as true at LATAM or Nubank as it was at KTB in 2017.
