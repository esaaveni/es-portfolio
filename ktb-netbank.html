Context

Krungthai Bank (KTB) is one of Thailand’s largest banks, state-owned and serving tens of millions of customers. In 2017 the bank was rebuilding its NetBank mobile experience, and the strategy agency DINN had delivered the upstream work: customer research, strategic direction, and a brand foundation.

Research reports don’t move money. Someone has to translate strategic findings into an information architecture, screen flows, interaction rules, and a system developers can build — and then prove with real users that the translation worked. That was our job.

The handoff gap

The gap between “research says users want simpler payments” and a payment flow a Thai grandmother can complete is where most bank app redesigns die. The research was solid; what didn’t exist yet was the structural decisions: what lives on the home screen, how accounts, payments, and products relate, what happens at every error state.

We treated DINN’s research as constraints, not suggestions — every architecture decision had to trace back to a finding. Where the research was silent, we marked it as an assumption to be tested rather than guessing silently. The usability protocol later in the project existed precisely to catch those marked assumptions.

Architecture

The new information architecture was drafted by hand, on paper, against the research — balance and filtering, payments, products, and a “Dreams” section for savings goals where the bank suggests amounts from the user’s actual cash flow. Sketching the map before any screen design forced the structural argument to happen early, when changing it was free.

Flow design — Send Money

Send Money was the highest-stakes flow in the app — the one customers use most and trust least. The flow covered sending to other people, to the user’s own accounts, adding accounts, and scheduling transactions, with every screen state and decision branch documented in a single annotated diagram.

The diagram was the contract between design and development. Each annotation answered a question a developer would otherwise have to guess: what happens when the recipient isn’t found, when the amount exceeds the balance, when the user edits a saved contact mid-flow.

Validation

We wrote and ran a two-day moderated usability protocol against the working design. Day one: icon findability, registration, login, look and feel, sending money to others, and payments. Day two: transfers to own accounts — including adding an account and scheduling a transaction — top-up, and paying from favorites.

Findings were graded on a declared severity scale before testing began:

Critical

The app doesn’t work if this is ignored. Must be solved.

Important

It works, but it’s genuinely difficult to use. Solve to improve performance and satisfaction.

Basic

Doesn’t affect functionality, but annoys users and erodes satisfaction.

Measured outcomes per task: completion rate, average time, and a satisfaction questionnaire covering label comprehension, language, navigation flow, interactive elements, and system feedback. A second round of testing focused specifically on the critical issues from round one and verified the design enhancements resolved them.

System

Two guideline sets made the work survive its handoff. An icon system built on the Material Design grid — product icons, system icons, light and dark background variants — and a system feedback specification: what the app says, and how, at every error, pending, and success state.

Takeaways

Research is a constraint system

Treating the strategy agency’s findings as constraints — every IA decision traceable to a finding, every gap marked as a testable assumption — is what kept the translation honest. The alternative is decorating your own opinions with someone else’s research.

Declare severity before you test

Agreeing on what “critical” means before seeing any findings removed the negotiation afterward. The same discipline — define the evaluation before the evidence exists — became the backbone of my experimentation practice years later.

Error states are the product

In banking, the success path is table stakes — trust is decided in the pending screens, failed transfers, and recovery paths. Specifying system feedback as a first-class deliverable, not a development afterthought, is the difference between an app that works and one people trust with their salary.
