The Interactive Touch Table

An end-to-end self-service retail experience — hardware, software, and the store around them

Context

True Group is the second-largest telecom in Thailand. TrueMove H — our client — generated 72% of the group’s 2018 revenue and added two million subscribers that year, reaching 29.2 million users. Their flagship stores carried an interactive touch table meant to be the centerpiece of the retail experience.

True came to Lead Innovation with a deceptively simple brief: create a “wow” self-service in-store experience that helps people decide which phone to buy, brings in new customers, and takes load off the floor staff. For Lead Innovation, it was also a strategic bet — a door into a new market.

What made this project different from a screen redesign: the product was a piece of furniture. The design surface included the hardware, the software, the store layout, and the bodies of the people standing around it.

Problem

The existing table didn’t work — not as software, and not as a place. Watching it in the store, the failure had five distinct layers:

Location · isolated

The table sat away from the natural path customers walked. People didn’t avoid it — they never reached it.

Ergonomics · oversized

At 120 cm deep, the far side of the screen was physically out of reach. Content lived where hands couldn’t go.

Interactions · disconnected

Touch targets responded to no recognizable logic. Nothing on the table related to the physical phones on display two meters away.

Content · uninteresting

Static spec sheets — the same information printed on the shelf card, now requiring more effort to read.

Performance · poor

Lag long enough that users assumed the table was broken and walked away.

Research

We started in the stores, not in the office. Direct observation: how people moved through the space, what they did with their hands, where they stopped, what made them lean in or walk past. The target population was concentrated — young adults 18–29 and middle youth 30–44 made up 64% of prepaid customers and potential new subscribers.

The ergonomic analysis did the heaviest lifting. Mapping reach distances on the existing table against Fitts’s Law made the core failure measurable: the distance and size of targets meant the table was slow by geometry, before any software ran. No interface redesign could fix a surface the human arm couldn’t serve.

This reframed the brief. The client asked for better content on the table. The research said the table itself — its size, position, and relationship to the physical phones — was the product.

Solution

concept

A self-service touch point built around the physical phone — place a real device on the table

and

the table recognizes it and surfaces its story: camera, battery, performance — compare two phones side by side by placing them side by side.

The concept inverted the old table’s logic. Instead of asking customers to navigate menus to find a phone they were already holding, the phone itself became the controller. Object recognition through conductive markers let the table know which device was on the glass and where.

We scoped it in two phases. Phase one: the features people actually compare when buying a phone — camera, battery, screen, performance. Phase two: tailor-made sales packages built from the customer’s selection. This kept the first release focused on the decision moment, not the full sales funnel.

Ergonomics drove the physical spec: a narrower table, content zones within natural reach, and interaction areas placed where two people could stand side by side — because phone buying, we saw in the stores, is rarely a solo activity.

Prototyping

Before any code, we built the table out of paper. A 1:1 board mock of the surface, paper radial menus, real phones as props — and people walking up to it cold. Rapid paper prototyping identified seven distinct interaction patterns users naturally tried, including the two that survived to production: compare by proximity (two phones placed near each other trigger comparison) and compare on full screen.

Testing at full physical scale mattered more than visual fidelity. A clickable on-screen prototype can’t tell you that a control sits four centimeters past a comfortable reach, or that two users sharing the table block each other’s zones. The paper table could.

UI design

The main UI challenge wasn’t visual — it was structural. Phone lineups change every few weeks, each device ships with different specs and marketing content, and the table had to absorb all of it without a redesign. I built the interface as a layered system: gradient background, illustration, pattern, content, and control layers — each independently replaceable as devices rotated.

Radial menus anchored to where the phone sat — not to screen edges — so controls always appeared at the user’s hand. Designing for a horizontal 4K surface that two people read from opposite sides forced decisions no mobile pattern library covers: text orientation, ownership zones, and what happens when two interactions collide.

Execution — the breaking point

Object recognition was the heart of the concept, and it nearly killed the project. I was responsible for researching conductive materials, proposing hardware specifications, and overseeing the development process — territory far outside a typical product design role.

Midway through, the honest internal assessment was: “we might be involved in a project we could not handle.” The recognition markers — conductive pads the table senses through the glass — had to be precise to the millimeter, durable enough for retail abuse, and cheap enough to attach to every display phone. Off-the-shelf options didn’t exist.

We designed the markers ourselves — from hand-dimensioned sketches to the 3D model the producer manufactured from. Each marker’s contact-point geometry encoded which phone it carried, so the table knew the device the moment it touched the glass. Iterating on physical samples against the capacitive sensing was slow, unglamorous work, and it was the difference between a concept video and a product in a store.

Outcome

The table shipped. By mid-2019 it was live in True’s flagship store — positioned on the customer path this time, sized to human reach, with real phones driving the interaction.

In the Facebook interview deck I built a year later, I titled this case “story of a successful failure.” Both words are accurate. The product shipped and worked — people walked up, placed phones, compared, stayed. And the project repeatedly exceeded what we knew how to do, forcing us to either learn fast or scale back. Phase two — the tailor-made sales packages — never shipped; the recognition hardware consumed the budget and the calendar.

This project predates my experimentation practice — there was no A/B framework, no instrumented funnel. The evidence here is the shipped product and the process that got it there. The discipline of measuring came later; the discipline of diagnosing started here.

Takeaways

Ergonomics is the first interface

The original table failed by geometry before it failed by software. Reach, height, and position decided what was usable; pixels only operated inside that envelope. Since this project, “can the body do this comfortably?” comes before “is this screen clear?”

Prototype at the scale of the problem

Paper radial menus on a 1:1 board found issues no high-fidelity screen prototype could: blocked reach zones, two-user collisions, controls drifting out of hand range. Fidelity should match the riskiest dimension — here, that dimension was physical.

Feasibility is a design material

“We might be involved in a project we could not handle” was the most useful sentence anyone said. Naming it forced the phase split, the descope of phase two, and the hand-built marker spec. Treating feasibility as something to design with — not something engineering reports back later — is what got the table out of the lab.
